## Question            <!-- agent-drafted, confirm -->
(drafted; check) This is a review article. It asks what drives tropical deforestation, whether observed forest loss departs from the socially optimal level, and which policies can align deforestation decisions with their social costs (pp. 725-726). The review focuses on the "third wave" of research, which is built on high-resolution satellite data and microeconometric or structural methods (pp. 725-726).

## Setting and data    <!-- agent-drafted, confirm -->
(drafted; check) The scope is the tropics, with most of the evidence from Brazil and Indonesia and some from the DRC, Mexico, Uganda, Benin and others.

- Motivating facts (pp. 724-725): 1.48 million km² of tropical forest was lost in 2001-2020. The tropics account for more than half of global forest loss over that period. Deforestation contributes an estimated 6-17% of anthropogenic GHG emissions.
- Figures 1-2 use Hansen et al. (2013) Global Forest Change, defining forest as 50% tree cover and reporting gross loss.
- Section 2 (pp. 727-730) surveys measurement:
  - Sensors: Landsat (30 m, 16-day revisit), MODIS (250 m, daily since 2000), AVHRR, Sentinel-2 (10 m), and radar/lidar.
  - Products: Hansen GFC, Song et al. (2018), Vancutsem et al. (2021), and Brazil-specific series.
  - Measurement problems: cloud cover; selective logging and degradation that are hard to detect; nonclassical, geographically correlated error (Alix-Garcia & Millimet 2022); bias in two-way FE with pixel FE when loss is a binary, irreversible outcome (Garcia & Heilmayr 2022); and pixel-versus-decision-unit aggregation (Avelino et al. 2016).
- Satellite systems are also used for enforcement, for example Brazil's DETER (pp. 727-728).

## Identification      <!-- agent-drafted, confirm -->
(drafted; check) The review does no estimation of its own. It organizes the literature around benchmark models of resource extraction:

- Hotelling (exhaustible) and a sole-owner renewable-resource model (Section 3; Supplemental Appendix A).
- Four extensions (Section 4):
  - alternative land uses (4.1)
  - unpriced externalities, with a Pigouvian/PES model in Supplemental Appendix B (4.2)
  - common-property and weak property rights, with free-entry overexploitation in Supplemental Appendix C (4.3)
  - political economy (4.4)

For each extension it gives the theory and then the empirical evidence. The designs it reports include:

- price-times-exposure regressions
- event studies on staggered treatment timing (trade agreements)
- RCTs (PES in Uganda, land registration in Benin, CDD in The Gambia)
- a spatial RD at Brazil's national border
- IV, using cloud cover as an instrument for DETER-driven enforcement
- panel DiD on election timing
- structural dynamic discrete-choice models of land use (Scott 2014, Souza-Rodrigues 2019, Araujo et al. 2022, Hsiao 2022, Dominguez-Iino 2023)

## Main result         <!-- agent-drafted, confirm; include magnitudes -->
(drafted; check) The review's conclusions, with the magnitudes it reports:

- **Land-use returns drive clearing.** Higher agricultural prices and market access raise deforestation (Section 4.1). Regional trade agreements raised deforestation by 19-26% cumulatively over three years (Abman & Lundberg 2020, p. 734). Brazil's 2008 rural-credit conditionality reduced cumulative deforestation by up to 60% over nine years (Assunção et al. 2020, p. 736).
- **Externality instruments work unevenly.** In Uganda, PES cut deforestation from 9.1% to 4.2% of baseline forest cover, with no leakage (Jayachandran et al. 2017, p. 738). An Indonesian fire-free PES program had no detectable effect (p. 738).
- The estimated effects of protected areas are "generally modest" (pp. 739-740). Zero-deforestation cattle agreements had no aggregate effect, while the Amazon Soy Moratorium reduced deforestation without leakage (pp. 740-741).
- **Property-rights evidence is "surprisingly mixed"** (p. 742):
  - Land registration in Benin: -20% forest loss and -5% fires (p. 742).
  - Brazilian titling: more deforestation among small and medium holders (pp. 742-743).
  - Insecure tenure in Nicaragua: 14% more forest cover (p. 743).
  - Indigenous land demarcation in the Amazon: up to -75% (Baragwanath & Bayi 2020, p. 743).
- **Political economy is "often of first-order importance"** (p. 727). In Section 4.4:
  - National policy: Brazil's side of the border deforested 3-4 times more in 2001-2005; the gap closed in 2006 and reappeared in 2014 (p. 745).
  - Brazil's Priority List: -43% deforestation in listed municipalities (p. 745).
  - DETER: a 50% rise in enforcement lowered municipal deforestation by 25% (p. 746).
  - Electoral cycles: deforestation was 8-10% higher in election years where the incumbent mayor ran again (Brazil 2002-2012, Pailler 2018, p. 746). Indonesian district-head elections show more deforestation in the year before the election (Burgess et al. 2011 WP; Cisneros et al. 2021; no magnitude given). Fires fall in election years (Balboni et al. 2021) (p. 746).
  - Jurisdiction splits: +8.2% provincial deforestation per extra district and about -3.3% timber price (Burgess et al. 2012, p. 747).
- **Open areas the review flags** (pp. 747-749): measuring conservation value, scaling PES and Pigouvian instruments, enforcement under low state capacity, local politicians' incentives and capture, cross-border policy design, and the DRC and sub-Saharan Africa.
