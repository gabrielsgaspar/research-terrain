## Question            <!-- agent-drafted, confirm -->

(drafted; check) A review in the Journal of Economic Perspectives of the environmental justice literature, with emphasis on economists' work. Its questions: how robust is the finding that low-income households and people of color are more exposed to pollution, given different exposure measures, spatial units and controls? And which mechanisms produce that pattern? It argues that how much weight each mechanism carries is still unclear, and that much prior work uses methods that are "inherently indecisive" (pp. 188–189).

## Setting and data    <!-- agent-drafted, confirm -->

(drafted; check) A review with no new estimation. The focus is "almost exclusively" on the US. It notes that similar questions arise elsewhere, including land use, forest preservation, international toxic and e-waste disposal, and climate change (p. 189).

- Motivating case: the 1982 siting of the PCB landfill in Warren County, North Carolina. Warren was 60% black and had 25% of families below the poverty line, against 27% and 6% in the alternative, Chatham County (p. 185).
- Illustrative descriptive figures: 2010 Toxic Release Inventory (TRI) emissions in North Carolina mapped against census-tract share non-Hispanic white (Fig. 1) and per capita income (Figs. 2–3) (pp. 186–188).
- The literature reviewed uses hazardous waste sites, landfills, TRI facilities, Superfund sites, emissions weighted by toxicity, and air-dispersion models (p. 189, fns. 2–5).

## Identification      <!-- agent-drafted, confirm -->

(drafted; check) The review is organised in three parts.

1. **Measuring exposure (pp. 189–193).**
   - Proximity versus emissions, toxicity and dispersion-based measures.
   - Whether to add controls. The authors argue this depends on the question: yes when looking for social causes, not necessarily when establishing that an inequity exists.
   - Spatial assignment. They argue "unit-hazard coincidence" (counting exposure only within the nuisance's own tract or zip code) has three problems, and conclude that distance-based measures are superior (pp. 190–191).
   - Spatial scale and the ecological fallacy (Fig. 4, pp. 191–193).
   - Whether disparate exposure leads to disparate health and education harms (p. 193).
2. **Four mechanisms (pp. 194–202).**
   - Disproportionate siting by firms (pp. 194–195), via taste-based discrimination, local factor costs correlated with demographics, or government steering.
   - "Coming to the nuisance": Tiebout sorting by households, with the "pyramid of environmental gentrification" (Fig. 5, Banzhaf and McCormick 2012) (pp. 195–198).
   - Coasean bargaining between firms and communities (pp. 198–201).
   - Political economy and government enforcement (pp. 201–202).
3. **Policy options (pp. 202–204).**

On the empirical designs it reviews, it notes the following.

- Difference-in-differences studies of demographic change after a facility is sited or closed give mixed results (fn. 6). It gives four reasons such tests can fail to detect sorting even when sorting drives the cross-sectional pattern: spatial general equilibrium effects; non-identification of individual sorting from aggregate population changes (Depro, Timmins and O'Neil 2015); hysteresis or stigma; and multiplier effects (pp. 197–198).
- For the political-economy channel, it treats collective-action proxies such as voter turnout as the common empirical measure (pp. 201–202).

## Main result         <!-- agent-drafted, confirm; include magnitudes -->

(drafted; check) Conclusions and the magnitudes the review reports:

- **Descriptive pattern, North Carolina TRI 2010 (p. 186).** The share of facilities in tracts more than 80% non-Hispanic white is about double the share in tracts more than 80% non-white (22% vs. 10%). But mostly-white tracts outnumber mostly-non-white tracts almost five to one (788 vs. 166). 63% of TRI facilities are in tracts with per capita income below $21,000.
- **Robustness of the correlation.**
  - The finding of greater exposure for low-income households and people of color is "broadly supported" across exposure measures (p. 189).
  - The United Church of Christ follow-up (Bullard et al. 2007) finds racial disparities in hazardous-waste location "largely unchanged" 20 years on (pp. 189–190).
  - Race is more likely to stay significant with distance-based measures (Mohai and Saha 2006; Ringquist 2005) (p. 191).
  - Baden, Noonan and Turaga (2007) survey 110 studies and find inequities stronger at smaller units of analysis (p. 192).
  - In individual birth data (about 11 million births, five states, 1989–2003), black mothers are 0.7 and 5.3 percentage points more likely to live near Superfund and TRI sites, and Hispanic mothers 0 and 4.0 points (Currie, Greenstone and Moretti 2011) (pp. 192–193).
  - The authors conclude that the ecological fallacy tends to mask injustice in aggregate data rather than create it (p. 193).
- **Harms (p. 193).** The county-level effect of large changes in total suspended particulates on infant mortality is 1.6 times larger for blacks than for whites (Chay and Greenstone 2003).
- **Siting (p. 195).** At the time of siting, firms do go to areas with a disproportionate share of people of color. But Wolverton (2009) finds this reflects land, labour and transport costs more than demographics directly.
- **Sorting (pp. 197–198).**
  - Cleanup of Superfund sites, brownfields and hazardous waste sites is estimated to raise housing prices by 5–15% (p. 197).
  - The authors conclude "the jury is still out" on coming to the nuisance (p. 198).
- **Coasean bargaining and politics (pp. 199–202).**
  - Kettleman City: a town 90% Hispanic, with 40% of residents speaking no English. The county was to receive $7 million a year across a 1,400 square mile county (p. 201).
  - Race and English-speaking are correlated with shale-gas lease terms after conditioning on income (Timmins and Vissing 2017) (p. 201).
  - Better-organised communities, proxied by voter turnout, are less likely to see expansion of hazardous-waste processing (Hamilton 1993, 1995) (p. 201).
  - Enforcement evidence is mixed on race. Collective-action measures predict enforcement. The authors conclude regulatory actions are "at least correlated with the political power of local communities" (p. 202).
- **Policy (pp. 202–204).**
  - Give local populations a seat at the table.
  - Pollution-targeted policy may have a progressive "double dividend."
  - If sorting dominates, people-based income policies may be more effective.
  - Benefit–cost practice that uses a constant willingness to pay can pass a policy that makes everyone worse off. Numerical example: rich willingness to pay $8m and poor $4m per statistical life, against an average of $6m (p. 204).
  - The authors stress that any prescription depends on which mechanism drives the inequity (p. 204).

## What it doesn't do  <!-- Gabriel -->
(drafted by Claude Code; Gabriel to rewrite)

- It is about the US and about pollution siting. Land use and forests get one
  sentence (p. 189), and it estimates nothing itself.
- It measures the political-economy channel with collective-action proxies
  such as turnout, not with who holds office, and it has no electoral
  discontinuity.
- Environmental justice is about exposed people. Land with no residents, the
  focus of our displacement hypothesis, is outside its frame.

## Relevance to us     <!-- Gabriel: what we borrow, how we differ -->
(drafted by Claude Code; Gabriel to rewrite)

- Its framing is ours: harm goes where political power is weak (p. 202).
  Clearing pushed onto land with no voters is the extreme case.
- We borrow its exposure lessons. Distance-based measures beat counting only
  the hazard's own unit (pp. 190-191), which is why decision 0004 uses a
  distance-decay exposure rather than catchment membership. Results depend on
  spatial scale, which is why we keep 1 km cells and report 2 and 5 km rings.
- Sorting ("coming to the nuisance") confounds cross-sectional patterns. In
  our design, sorting can shape where strongholds are, but not which side of a
  close race wins, so it changes who is exposed, not the comparison.
- How we differ: we test one mechanism, representation, with quasi-random
  variation, instead of describing exposure gaps.
