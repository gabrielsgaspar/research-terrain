## Question            <!-- agent-drafted, confirm -->
(drafted; check) Does the gender of a local leader change which public goods get provided? The paper uses India's mandated, randomly assigned reservation of village-council head (Pradhan) seats for women. It tests whether reserved councils invest differently, and whether the differences track women's revealed preferences (pp. 1409-1411). The benchmark is a citizen-candidate model with ex post elite lobbying (Section 3). Under that model, reservation can move policy, unlike in a Downsian or Coasian world (p. 1420).

## Setting and data    <!-- agent-drafted, confirm -->
(drafted; check) The 73rd Constitutional Amendment (1992-93) devolved spending on local infrastructure to Gram Panchayats (GPs) and reserved one-third of Pradhan posts for women (pp. 1410-1413).

- **GPs:** about 10,000 people across 5-15 villages each. The council votes by majority, and the Pradhan is the only full-time member (p. 1412).
- **Funding:** mainly state schemes, chiefly the JRY for infrastructure, plus drinking water, welfare and functioning grants (p. 1412). JRY is 30% of GP income in 40 West Bengal balance sheets (fn. 5).
- **Two districts (Section 4.1, pp. 1423-1425):**
  - **Birbhum, West Bengal.** All 166 GPs were surveyed in summer 2000. Five were used for pretesting, leaving **161 GPs: 54 reserved, 107 unreserved** (Table I, p. 1414). Pradhan reservation began with the May 1998 election, and West Bengal has had elected Panchayats since 1978. For each GP the authors ran a Pradhan interview plus a survey of three villages: two random villages and the Pradhan's own. The survey included a participatory resource map of all infrastructure built or repaired since May 1998. Village-level regressions use only the two random villages, giving 322 village observations (Table V notes).
  - **Udaipur, Rajasthan.** **100 hamlets** were sampled in Aug-Dec 2002, after the first draft (40 reserved, 60 unreserved; Table I). Villages were drawn with size-weighted probability from a subset covered by a local NGO, then one hamlet per village (fn. 17). The investment reference period is 2000-2002. Only village-level data were collected, with no Pradhan interview. Elected Panchayats there date only from 1995, and the 2000 election was the second reservation cycle.
- **Preference proxy:** formal requests and complaints that women and men brought to the GP in the previous six months (pp. 1421-1422). In West Bengal, men's complaints come from a random resurvey of 48 villages (fn. 24).
- **Compliance:** all Pradhans in reserved GPs are women. In unreserved GPs, 6.5% are women in West Bengal and 1.7% in Rajasthan, which is one woman (Table I, p. 1414).

## Identification      <!-- agent-drafted, confirm -->
(drafted; check)
- **Assignment:** within each district, GPs are listed by a pre-existing legislative serial number. They are split into SC-, ST- and unreserved lists, and every third GP from the first on each list is reserved for a woman. Positions rotate in later elections (pp. 1413-1414, fn. 6). The authors reconstructed the full reserved list for Birbhum and say this verifies that allocation followed the rule. They could not do this for Rajasthan (fn. 7). The randomisation is stratified by SC/ST reservation status (p. 1439).
- **Balance:** 1991 Census village characteristics show no significant differences between reserved and unreserved GPs. The joint test gives F = .93 (p = .53) in West Bengal and F = 1.54 (p = .11) in Rajasthan (Table II, p. 1424).
- **Estimand:** the reduced-form difference in means between reserved and unreserved GPs. The paper frames it as the effect of being reserved, within a system that has reservation, not a comparison of systems with and without reservation (pp. 1425-1426). The IV estimate for Pradhan gender would be the reduced form x 1.075 (fn. 20). In West Bengal, village-level SEs are Moulton-corrected for GP clustering.
- **Linking investment to preferences:** outcomes are standardised by the unreserved mean and SD, then stacked across goods. Investment is regressed on Reserved, on Reserved x D_i (women's minus men's request share for good i), and on good FE (eq. 1). Eq. 2 uses Reserved x S_i, the average request share. Eq. 3 adds village-specific complaint terms D_ij and S_ij to test whether female Pradhans respond more to local complaints (pp. 1426-1427).
- **Separating gender from other effects of reservation (West Bengal only; Section 6, pp. 1434-1440):**
  - Controls for all Pradhan characteristics interacted with D_i (Table VI col. 4).
  - Comparison with unreserved GPs whose previous Pradhan's council seat was reserved, so the Pradhan is also new (Table VIII col. 2).
  - Comparison with GPs that will be reserved in 2003, so the Pradhan is also a lame duck (col. 3).
  - Comparison within SC/ST-reserved GPs, where men and women have similar economic backgrounds (col. 4).
  - All three indicators interacted with D_i together (Table VI col. 5).

## Main result         <!-- agent-drafted, confirm; include magnitudes -->
(drafted; check) Reserved-minus-unreserved differences in public goods since the last election, by type (Table V, p. 1432). Standard errors are in parentheses.

**West Bengal, village level:**
- Drinking water facilities newly built or repaired: 23.83 vs. 14.74, difference **+9.09 (4.02)**.
- Roads in good condition: .41 vs. .23, **+.18 (.06)**.
- Panchayat-run informal education centres: .06 vs. .12, **-.06 (.04)**, significant only at 10%.
- Irrigation facilities: **-.38 (1.26)**, not significant.
- Other goods (ponds, biogas, sanitation, community buildings): +.32 (.48).
- Jointly significant: F = 4.15, p = .001.

**West Bengal, GP level:**
- New tubewell: +.07 (.03).
- Metal road built or repaired: **+.19 (.08)**.
- Informal education centre in the GP: **-.16 (.07)**.
- At least one irrigation pump: +.07 (.05).
- Jointly significant: F = 4.73, p = .001.

**Rajasthan (village/hamlet level):**
- Drinking water facilities: 7.31 vs. 4.69, **+2.62 (.95)**.
- Roads in good condition: .90 vs. .98, **-.08 (.04)**.
- Irrigation: -.02 (.06).
- Other goods: +.05 (.09).
- Jointly significant: F = 2.88, p = .02.

**Revealed preferences (Table IV, p. 1430):**
- In West Bengal, women raise drinking water and roads most often (.31 each among public-works issues). Men raise roads, irrigation (.20 vs. .04 for women), drinking water and education.
- In Rajasthan, women raise drinking water more than men (.54 vs. .43) and roads less (.13 vs. .23).
- The complaint distributions do not differ between reserved and unreserved GPs (chi-square p = .64 WB, .68 RJ).
- The paper reads the opposite-signed road effects across the two states as matching these gender gaps (p. 1431).

**Alignment with women's preferences (Table VI, p. 1433):**
- Reserved x D_i = **1.63 (.501)** in West Bengal and **4.40 (1.454)** in Rajasthan. That is, a 10-pp gap in women's minus men's request share raises provision of that good by **.16 SD** (WB) and **.44 SD** (RJ) in reserved GPs (p. 1431).
- Reserved x S_i = 2.04 (.642) WB and 1.78 (.728) RJ.
- Village-specific complaint terms (cols. 3 and 8): Reserved x D_ij = .03 (.047) WB and -.37 (.169) RJ; Reserved x S_ij = -.01 (.155) WB and .05 (.27) RJ. The text calls the D_ij and S_ij interactions not significant and concludes that female Pradhans are not more responsive to local complaints; their own preferences are closer to women's (p. 1434). Check: the RJ D_ij estimate as extracted (-.37, SE .169) has |t| of about 2.2.

**Participation (Table III, p. 1428):**
- In West Bengal, the women's share of Gram Samsad participants rises from 6.88% to 9.80% (+2.92, SE 1.44). The share of villages where women filed a complaint rises from .11 to .20 (+.09, SE .05).
- There is no effect in Rajasthan.

**Robustness (Table VI cols. 4-5; Table VIII, p. 1438):**
- The D_i interaction is essentially unchanged: 1.56 with Pradhan characteristics (col. 4) and 1.67 with all three reservation indicators (col. 5), versus 1.63 in the new-Pradhan and lame-duck subsamples and 1.54 in the SC/ST subsample.
- Water, roads and informal-school differences have similar magnitude in all subsamples.
- Female Pradhans in West Bengal are younger, less educated (-2.79 years), poorer, and less experienced: 11% vs. 43% previously on the GP council. 43% report spousal help (Table VII, p. 1435).
